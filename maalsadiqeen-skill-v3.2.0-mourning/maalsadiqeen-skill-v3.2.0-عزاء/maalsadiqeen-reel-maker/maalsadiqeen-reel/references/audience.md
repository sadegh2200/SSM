# Audience — who is actually watching

«مع الصادقين» speaks to Arabic-reading Muslims, 35–60, in Iraq, Lebanon, the Gulf and the diaspora.
They are not beginners and they are not scholars. The page's job is to be the place where a serious
question gets a calm, sourced answer — the opposite of the shouting they scroll past.

Every decision in this skill is downstream of this page. If a choice would please a 19-year-old
looking for a fight, it is probably wrong for this audience.

## 1 · Three readers

### R1 — the serious learner (≈ 50 %)

- 40–60, reads on a phone at night, often after a majlis or a lecture.
- Knows the famous ḥadīths; notices when a reference is vague or a sanad is skipped.
- Wants: the text, its source, and the meaning — in that order.
- Objection: «هذا كلام معروف، ما الجديد؟» → the answer is the *layer*, not the novelty: what the
  text says, what the scholar explains, what we infer.
- Reward: a reference they can save and a sentence they can repeat accurately.

### R2 — the educated seeker (≈ 30 %)

- 30–50, university-educated, reads Arabic and often a second language, prays but does not study.
- Wants: to understand a concept that religion keeps being used to answer badly.
- Objection: «هذا كلام قديم لا يمسّ حياتي» → the answer is the «الأثر في الحياة» line: one concrete
  change in the week, not a slogan.
- Reward: a distinction they did not have before (نصّ / شرح / استنباط).

### R3 — the diaspora (≈ 20 %)

- 25–45, second-generation, reads Arabic more slowly, often watches muted with captions.
- Wants: to belong without being lectured; to be able to send something to a parent or a friend.
- Objection: «لم أفهم المصطلح» → define the term on screen or in the first comment, in one line.
- Reward: something sendable — a verse + one sentence that travels well in a family chat.

## 2 · What they do on a phone

- Vertical, muted first; the sound comes after the first second if at all.
- One thumb, one minute, often one eye.
- They decide in 1–3 seconds whether this is "for them"; a face or a shout is usually a no, a
  beautiful line of Arabic is usually a yes.
- They send content that makes them look thoughtful to someone they respect.
- They comment when a term is unclear or a claim feels unsourced — those comments are the content
  plan for the next reel.

## 3 · What they reject

| rejected | why |
|---|---|
| polemic, naming sects or opponents | they came for knowledge, not a fight |
| fear-selling («ستموت غداً», «أنت في خطر») | it cheapens the page and the religion |
| clickbait («لن تصدّق», «السر الذي») | they have seen it used against them |
| dialect or slang | the page's register is classical; dialect reads as a different, younger brand |
| emoji and exclamation marks | the voice is calm; punctuation should not shout |
| unsourced certainty | they check; one invented reference costs a follower |
| long sermons | 40 seconds is the contract; a lecture belongs in a series |

## 4 · Language

- On screen: **formal Arabic, fully vocalised** where sacred or quoted; modern standard elsewhere.
- Persian: only in replies to a Persian-speaking user and in internal documents — never in a reel.
- Terms: define on first use (`المعية`, `الولاية`, `السند`) in ≤ 6 words or in comment slot 3.
- Honorifics: `عليه السلام` for the Imams, `صلّى الله عليه وآله` for the Prophet, `(ع)` where space is
  tight. Never omit the honourific for a Maʿṣūm, even in a tight line.

## 5 · What "good" looks like to them

> «وقفت عند كلمة، وفهمت لماذا قالها، وعرفت من أين جاءت، وأستطيع أن أرسلها لابني.»

One word, one reason, one source, one use. If a reel delivers four of those, it worked.
